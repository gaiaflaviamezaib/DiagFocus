# The science behind the games

ADHD is usually described through three groups of difficulties: inattention, impulsivity and hyperactivity. Underneath these, research points to weaker **executive functions**, the mental skills that let us hold back an automatic response, keep information in mind, filter distractions, and switch strategies (Barkley, 1997).

Each DiagFocus game adapts a classic test of one of these functions into a short, child-friendly format. None of them diagnoses ADHD alone. Together they are meant to give a clinician an objective profile to set beside interviews and rating scales.

## 1. Le Gardien de l'Espace: Go/No-Go

**Rule:** orbs appear at different places on screen. Tap them as fast as possible. Do not tap the red ones.

**Origin:** Go/No-Go tasks are closely related to continuous performance tests (CPTs), first introduced by Rosvold et al. (1956). Clinical tools such as TOVA and CPT-3 are computerised tests of this kind.

**What it measures:**
- Tapping a red orb (a *commission error*) reflects difficulty **inhibiting** a response, i.e. impulsivity.
- Missing a green orb (an *omission error*) reflects lapses of attention.
- Greater variability in reaction times is a frequently reported finding in ADHD.

## 2. Le Chef Cuistot: Corsi block-tapping

**Rule:** tiles in a grid light up one after another. Watch, then tap them back in the same order.

**Origin:** created by Philip Corsi at McGill University (Corsi, 1972), as the visual-spatial counterpart of the digit span test.

**What it measures:** **visuospatial working memory**, the ability to hold and update locations in mind. The score is usually the longest sequence reproduced correctly (the Corsi span).

## 3. Le Banc de Poissons: Eriksen flanker task

**Rule:** a row of five arrows (fish) appears. Blue arrows: answer with the direction of the **centre** one. Red arrows: answer with the direction of the **outer** ones.

**Origin:** Eriksen and Eriksen (1974). The classic task asks for the centre item only. DiagFocus adds a colour cue that changes which arrows to follow, which adds a rule-switching demand.

**What it measures:** **selective attention** and interference control. Responses are slower and less accurate when the flanking arrows point the other way (incongruent trials). The size of that slowdown is the flanker effect.

## 4. Le Gonfleur: Balloon Analogue Risk Task (BART)

**Rule:** each pump adds points to the balloon. Cash in to bank the points, but if the balloon pops first, they are lost. The balloon colours hide a secret.

**Origin:** Lejuez et al. (2002).

**What it measures:** **risk-taking and reward sensitivity**. The usual score is the average number of pumps on balloons that did not pop.

## 5. Le Labo des Potions: Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST)

**Rule:** sort each mystery card into one of three bins. The sorting rule is secret: the game only says *VRAI* or *FAUX*, and the rule can change without warning.

**Origin:** Berg (1948), later standardised as the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test.

**What it measures:** **cognitive flexibility**. The key measure is *perseveration*: continuing to apply the old rule after feedback shows it no longer works.

## Limits

- These are adaptations, not the validated clinical versions, so the scores need their own validation study and age norms before any clinical use.
- French HAS guidance on ADHD in children notes that tests such as the CPT can inform a clinical assessment but can neither confirm nor rule out the diagnosis. DiagFocus is designed to fit that role: screening and orientation, with the diagnosis left to the clinician.
- Performance also depends on fatigue, motivation, familiarity with screens and the testing environment.
